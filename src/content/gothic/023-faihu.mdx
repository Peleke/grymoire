Faihu means "cattle" — and therefore "wealth."

Before coinage, cattle were currency. They were portable (they walked), self-replicating (they bred), and universally valued (everyone needed meat and milk and leather). To count someone's wealth, you counted their herd. The word "pecuniary" comes from Latin *pecus* (cattle); "capital" from *caput* (head — as in head of cattle).

The Gothic *faihu* connects to English "fee" and German "Vieh" (livestock). The echo persists: we still pay fees, still exchange fees for services, still use a cattle-word for monetary transaction.

Numerically, Faihu carries 500. The half-thousand. Substantial accumulation — not just wealth but significant wealth, the kind that changes what's possible. Five hundred is enough to matter, enough to protect, enough to project power.

But Faihu also warns. Cattle die. Hoards scatter. The wealth that walks can walk away. The Germanic peoples had a saying preserved in the Hávamál: "Cattle die, kinsmen die, you yourself will die." Wealth is real but impermanent.

What is your *faihu*? What do you count to measure prosperity? And do you remember that all cattle, eventually, return to the earth?
