Þiuþ is pure Gothic invention.

Greek had no letter for the "th" sound (as in "think"), so Wulfila created one. He borrowed the shape from runic tradition — the thorn (þ) that still appears in Icelandic — and gave it a Gothic name meaning "good" or "the good."

This is significant. When faced with a gap in his source alphabet, Wulfila didn't force Greek to do what Greek couldn't do. He reached into his own tradition and made something new. Adaptation, not imitation.

Numerically, Þiuþ carries 9. The last single digit. The number before the turning, before 10 resets the count. Nine is completion at the edge: pregnant with the decimal that follows, full but not yet overflowing.

The name itself — "the good" — suggests moral weight. In an alphabet that would carry the Gospels, this letter embodied virtue itself. Every time a Gothic scribe wrote the "th" sound, they were writing a reminder: *be good*.
