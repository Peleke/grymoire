Jer means "year" — time measured not in days but in harvests.

For agricultural peoples, the year was not an abstraction but a lived rhythm: planting, tending, harvesting, resting. One *jer* was one complete cycle of work and reward, death and renewal. The calendar was written in grain.

The word survives in English "year" and German "Jahr." This is deep Proto-Germanic vocabulary, words so old they predate writing itself. When Wulfila gave this sound a letter, he was encoding something ancient.

Numerically, Jer carries 60. The Babylonian base. The number that structures our hours (60 minutes) and minutes (60 seconds). Sixty is how we divide time itself — not coincidentally, for a letter that means the largest natural unit of time.

The year contains all other cycles: the weeks within months, the seasons within the whole, the rhythm of work and rest that Dags (day) began. Jer is the container that holds them all.

Where are you in your current *jer*? Planting season or harvest? The fallow winter or the frantic growth of spring? The year invites you to notice where you stand in its turning.
