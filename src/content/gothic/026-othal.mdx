Oþal means "heritage" or "ancestral property" — the land passed down through generations.

This was not abstract ownership but living connection. The *oþal* was the farmstead your grandfather worked, the fields your great-grandmother knew, the soil that held the bones of your ancestors. To lose your *oþal* was to lose yourself; to hold it was to remain anchored in time.

The word survives in English "allodial" (land held in absolute ownership) and connects to German "Adel" (nobility). Heritage became aristocracy; the old farm became the manor. But the root meaning persists: that which is inherited, that which connects you to the dead.

Numerically, Oþal carries 800. Eight centuries. Enough time for a family to truly root, for place and people to become inseparable. Eight hundred years of working the same soil makes the soil part of you.

In modernity we move freely, often. We rent, we relocate, we leave our *oþal* behind. This is liberation and loss simultaneously. Mobility gives options; roots give meaning.

Where is your *oþal*? Not necessarily land — it might be a practice, a tradition, a way of being that was passed to you. What inheritance do you carry, and what will you pass on?
