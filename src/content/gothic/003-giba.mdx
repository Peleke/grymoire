Giba means "gift" — and in the Germanic world, gifts were never simple.

A gift creates obligation. A gift received must be repaid. The exchange weaves giver and receiver into a web of reciprocity that structures society itself. This is the logic of *giba*: generosity as social technology.

With the numeric value 3, Giba completes the first triangle of the Gothic number system. One is unity. Two is division. Three is synthesis — the first shape that encloses space, the first number that creates structure.

The word survives in modern English "give," stripped of its ceremonial weight but retaining its core meaning. When you give, you perform an ancient rite, even if you've forgotten the ritual.

Wulfila used this letter throughout his translation of the Gospels, a text obsessed with gifts: grace freely given, salvation unearned, bread broken and shared. The Gothic Christians would have heard in *giba* echoes of their older ways, gift-economy and grace-economy overlapping.

The best gifts are the ones that obligate you to become worthy of them.
