## Commentary



- Fate + Destiny 
- Fates of Men <-> Norns 
- Norns <-> Yggdrasil 
- Yggdrasil <-> Mimisbrunnr
- Mimisbrunnr <-> Odin's eye + Jotunheimar 

### The Three Voices

The *Gylfaginning* runs on a conceit: Gangleri — the wanderer, the questioner, probably Odin in disguise questioning himself — sits before three figures on three thrones. Hár (High), Jafnhár (Just-as-High), Þriði (Third). Three aspects of one authority, maybe. Three voices that correct and expand on each other.

Watch how the correction works here. Gangleri asks about the Gods. Hár answers directly: twelve, divine in nature. Done. Next question. But Jafnhár cuts in before Gangleri can continue — *the Goddesses are no less holy, and no less mighty.* It's not an addendum. It's a correction. Hár gave an incomplete answer, and Jafnhár won't let it stand.

This matters structurally. Snorri could have had Hár say "twelve Gods and many Goddesses." He didn't. He staged a correction — one voice interrupting another to insist on something the first voice omitted. The Goddesses don't get mentioned as an afterthought. They get mentioned because someone noticed the afterthought and refused it.

### She Knows, She Says Nothing

Then Third speaks, and the passage pivots from the collective to the specific: Frigg.

The characterization is extraordinary in its compression. Odin rules all things. The gods serve him like children serve a father. And Frigg — his wife — *veit hon örlög manna þótt hon segi eigi spár.* She knows the fates of men, though she speaks no prophecies.

Not *cannot* speak. Not *does not know how*. She knows and chooses silence.

This is a fundamentally different kind of power than Odin's. Odin trades an eye for knowledge at Mímir's well. He hangs nine nights on Yggdrasill to seize the runes. He wanders the world in disguise, asking questions, hoarding answers. His knowledge is *acquisitive* — taken, won, extracted at great cost, and then *used.* He speaks. He decrees. He manipulates.

Frigg just... knows. The verb is *vita* — to know, in the deep sense. Not to learn, not to discover. To know as a state of being. And she *segi eigi spár* — speaks no prophecy. The word *spá* carries weight: it's the root of *völva* (seeress), the whole prophetic tradition. Frigg has the knowledge that the völvur speak, but she herself does not perform it. She is not in the business of telling you your fate.

The Lokasenna stanza Snorri borrows to support this is Odin's own testimony. He's in the middle of a flyting — a ritual insult contest — defending his wife against Loki's provocations. And his defense isn't "she's faithful" or "she's powerful." It's: *she knows everything, and she tells no one.* He says it like it's the most impressive thing about her. Like her silence is what makes her dangerous.

### The Borrowed Stanza

A note on the sourcing, since it's worth flagging: the stanza Snorri quotes doesn't map cleanly onto any single verse in the *Lokasenna* as we have it. Stanza 21 in modern editions is Frigg speaking to Loki, not Odin speaking about Frigg. Stanza 29 is closer — Odin telling Loki to shut up — but the wording diverges.

This is normal for Snorri. He's writing in the 1220s, working from manuscripts and memory and oral tradition, and he quotes freely — sometimes verbatim, sometimes paraphrasing, sometimes stitching two stanzas together into a composite that serves his argument. The *Lokasenna* he had access to may not be identical to the *Lokasenna* in the *Codex Regius* (compiled ~1270, forty-some years later). Or he's doing what good writers do: reshaping his source to make the point land harder.

Either way, the rhetorical function is clear. He wants you to hear this in Odin's voice. The All-Father himself, mid-argument, mid-insult-match, pausing to say: *my wife knows the fates of every man alive, and she has never once told anyone.* That's not a defense. That's awe.

## Vocabulary

- **ásynjur** (f. pl.) — the Goddesses, specifically the female Æsir. Singular *ásynja*. From *áss* (god) + feminine suffix. Jafnhár's word choice: not "wives of the gods" or "divine women" but *ásynjur* — the same rank, the same root, the same category.
- **guðkunnigr** (adj.) — "divine in nature," literally "god-kin" or "of divine kind." *Guð* (god) + *kunnigr* (knowing, of a kind). Hár's descriptor for the twelve Æsir.
- **örlög** (n. pl.) — fates, destinies. Literally "primal layers" or "original laws" — *ör-* (primal, original) + *lög* (laws, layers). The word carries geological weight: these aren't decisions, they're strata. What has been laid down and cannot be unlaid.
- **spá** (f.) — prophecy, the act of prophetic speech. Root of *spákona* (prophetess) and *völva* (seeress, lit. "staff-bearer" but functionally: the one who speaks *spá*). Frigg has the knowledge but does not perform the *spá*.
- **æztr** (adj. superl.) — highest, foremost. Superlative of a lost positive form. Applied to Odin: he is not merely high — he is the *most* high.
- **ærr** (adj.) — mad, frenzied, out of one's mind. Odin's opening shot at Loki in the borrowed stanza. Not "angry" — genuinely unhinged. The Norse insult tradition doesn't mince.
- **legskaþu** (contraction) — *legska þú*: imperative "lay off" / "quit it." From *leggjask* (to lay off, to stop). *Quit it, Loki.*
- **trúa á** — to believe in, to trust in, to have faith in. Gangleri's verb for the relationship between men and gods. See the translation note on "worship" — the English flattens what the Norse leaves more open.
